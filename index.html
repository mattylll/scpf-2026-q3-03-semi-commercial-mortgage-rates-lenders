Semi-Commercial Mortgage Rates and Lenders in 2026

Two offers land on the same morning for the same building, a hardware shop with two flats above in a Staffordshire town, loan of 300,000 pounds. Offer A is at 6.9 percent a year with a 2 percent arrangement fee. Offer B is at 7.1 percent with a 1 percent fee. The landlord’s instinct is that A is cheaper because the rate is lower. Over five years, interest on A is 103,500 pounds plus a 6,000 pound fee, 109,500 in all. Interest on B is 106,500 plus a 3,000 pound fee, also 109,500. They cost exactly the same over five years, and if he sells or refinances after three, B is 1,200 pounds cheaper. That is the first lesson about semi-commercial mortgage rates: the headline number is only one of the moving parts. The second lesson is that the number itself is not a single thing but a base rate plus a margin, and the two are set by different people for different reasons.

How a semi-commercial rate is built

A rate is a margin plus a base rate. The base rate is the same for everyone. The margin is the lender’s opinion of your building.

The reference rate for most semi-commercial lending is the Bank of England base rate, 3.75 percent as of the 30 July 2026 decision, or a bank’s own base rate that tracks it. On top of that the lender adds a margin. On the mid 2026 bands, investment semi-commercial mortgages price at 6.5 to 8.5 percent a year across our lender panel, which means the margins in play run from roughly 2.75 to 4.75 percentage points over base. That two-point spread between the keenest and the widest margin is where all the underwriting happens.

A minority of lenders offer a fixed rate for an initial period of two to five years instead. The fix removes the base rate risk for that term, at a small premium over the equivalent variable margin, and usually carries an early repayment charge.

What moves the margin

Six things decide where a case lands inside the 6.5 to 8.5 percent band. In rough order of weight:

  1. Loan to value. A loan at 60 percent of value prices below one at the 70 to 75 percent ceiling, often by half a point or more. Every extra pound of deposit buys margin.
  2. The commercial covenant. An optician on a ten-year lease with eight years left is a different risk from a vape shop on a six-month licence. Lease length, the trade and the tenant’s accounts all feed in.
  3. The residential part. A let flat on a standard tenancy with its own front door supports the rate; a vacant flat, a shared entrance through the shop, or accommodation that fails a licensing test pushes it up.
  4. The split and the use class. A building close to the 40 percent residential line, or a flat above a takeaway, a pub or a betting shop, narrows the panel and lifts the margin.
  5. Investment or owner-occupier. Where the borrower trades from the shop, owner-occupier semi-commercial pricing sits at 6.0 to 7.5 percent, because the lender takes comfort from a business that needs the premises.
  6. The borrower. Landlord experience, a clean credit file and a sensible structure all tighten the margin. A first purchase through a company formed last month does the opposite.

Improve any one of these and the margin moves. Improve loan to value and the covenant together and a case can drop from the top of the band to the bottom.

Fees, and the true cost of a rate

The rate is not the cost. The hardware shop example above shows why: a 1 percent difference in arrangement fee on 300,000 pounds is 3,000 pounds, which offsets 0.2 percentage points of rate over five years and 0.4 points over two and a half. The lender camps and how each prices

Semi-commercial property is funded by three groups, and each builds its margin differently. We do not name individual lenders here; the money site does, and names change appetite from one quarter to the next.

High street banks lend on mixed-use property through their commercial arms. They price toward the bottom of the 6.5 to 8.5 percent band, but only for a narrow profile: an established commercial tenant on a long lease, a let flat, an experienced borrower, and loan to value nearer 70 percent than 75. Their margins are keen because their credit box is tight, and they are slow to flex on anything outside it.

Challenger banks and specialist semi-commercial lenders price across the middle and upper half of the band. In exchange they take on the cases a high street desk declines: an SPV with no track record, a part-vacant unit, a licensed use, a portfolio landlord with a dozen buildings, a borrower with historic adverse credit. They reach the full 75 percent loan to value, decide faster, and many of them accept business through intermediaries only, so a borrower ringing round will never find them.

Bridging lenders price by the month rather than the year, at about 0.70 to 0.95 percent a month, which annualises to 8.5 to 11 percent, over 1 to 24 months. They fund the building that does not yet qualify for a term loan, because the shop is empty or the flats need work, and their exit is a semi-commercial mortgage from one of the first two camps once the income is in place.

The right camp is decided by the building, not by the rate table. A clean asset placed with a specialist pays for flexibility it does not need; a complex asset sent to a high street bank gets declined after the valuation fee has been spent.

Running the case across the panel

Going to one bank means taking one bank’s margin and one bank’s view of the split. Our job is to package the case once, with the leases, the rent roll, the split measured by floor area and value, the deposit evidenced and the structure set, and run it across the whole lender panel so the offers come back side by side. On a straightforward shop and flat that usually produces two or three competing terms within the 6.5 to 8.5 percent band; on a harder case it identifies the one or two desks that will actually lend, before any application is made and any credit search is recorded.

2026 outlook

The Bank of England has held base rate at 3.75 percent since its 30 July 2026 decision, and the next announcement is due on 17 September 2026. Because nearly every semi-commercial mortgage is priced as a margin over a reference rate, a quarter-point cut would move variable pay rates inside a month and would also ease the stress rates lenders test rent against, lifting the loan the same building supports. Margins themselves have been stable through 2026; competition among specialist lenders has kept the top of the band from drifting up even as some high street desks have pulled back from secondary retail. Borrowers who expect base rate to fall are taking variable margins; those who need certainty on cashflow are fixing for two or three years and accepting the small premium.

FAQ

What is a good semi-commercial mortgage rate in 2026? On the mid 2026 bands, an investment case at 6.5 to 7.0 percent a year is toward the keen end and needs a low loan to value, a strong commercial tenant and an experienced borrower. Owner-occupier cases can sit at 6.0 to 7.5 percent. Anything in the 6.5 to 8.5 percent band is within the published market range.

Are semi-commercial mortgage rates fixed or variable? Mostly variable, priced as a margin over the Bank of England base rate or a lender’s own base rate. Some lenders fix for two to five years at a small premium, usually with an early repayment charge for the fixed term.

Why is my semi-commercial rate higher than a buy-to-let rate? Because a semi-commercial mortgage is underwritten as a commercial facility on a building with a trading unit, and the lender carries the commercial covenant risk. It is usually cheaper than a pure commercial mortgage on the same building, because the residential part lowers the risk, but dearer than a wholly residential buy-to-let.

Can I go direct to a semi-commercial lender? High street banks will take a direct approach. Many challenger and specialist semi-commercial lenders work through intermediaries only, so a broker is the only route to the full panel and to the desks that price the harder cases.
